## 1. Typical gross margin, return rate, repeat-purchase rate and AOV by category

### Takeaway
Across categories, the online winners pair high gross margin with low return friction and a natural reason to rebuy. Beauty, supplements, pets and consumables come closest on returns and repurchase. Apparel has high gross margin (about 57% for public apparel companies), but it also has the highest return rates (about 19–25% online, plus apparel-specific figures in the 23–25% range) and only middling repeat rates (about 24–26%). Apparel winners therefore have to engineer repeat purchase and reduce fit risk on purpose. Home/furniture and electronics carry lower margins (about 30–39%) and low repeat rates (about 15–18%).

### Inferences
- **Synthesized category scorecard** (directional; built from the figures above, which are mostly vendor data):

  | Category | Gross margin (proxy) | Online returns | Repeat / retention | AOV (Decile Q1'25, unconfirmed) | Net read |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Apparel | High (~57%) | Highest (~23–25%; 25–40% upper range) | Mid (~24–26%) | ~$191 | Margin funds ads; returns and fit risk are the main problem |
  | Footwear | Mid-high (~44%) | High (17–30%) | n/a | n/a | Fit risk plus lower margin than apparel |
  | Beauty | High (~51% proxy) | Very low (4–5%) | Mid (~26%) | ~$151 | Close to ideal online category; leads TikTok Shop |
  | Supplements / health | Very high (pharma proxy 72%) | Low (5–10%) | Higher (~29%) | ~$70 | Replenishment plus subscription; low AOV |
  | Pets | n/a | n/a | Higher (30%+) | Low (France €35) | Replenishment; Chewy-type scale player |
  | Home / furniture | Low (~30%) | Mid (8–15%) | Low (~15%) | Highest (~$266) | Big tickets, rare repurchase, heavy shipping |
  | Electronics | Mid (~39%), negative operating margin | Low (8–10%) | Low (~18%) | n/a | Commoditized and dominated by marketplaces |
  | Food & beverage | Low-mid (23–55% by segment) | Very low | Highest intent | ~$69 | Frequent purchase, but thin unit economics and high subscription churn early |

- Categories that produce the most DTC winners combine three things: margin of 50% or more to fund paid acquisition, low or manageable returns, and a natural repurchase cycle. Beauty and supplements fit best. Apparel has the margin but must solve returns (fit) and repeat purchase (reasons to come back). That is why apparel winners cluster around fit-consistent basics.
- Subscription works best in consumables. Apparel's low first-renewal churn on Recharge (16.1%) suggests that subscribers to a basics replenishment offer are sticky, but the base is thin, so treat this as a hypothesis, not a benchmark.
- Apparel's net margin (3.85% for public companies) is far below its gross margin (56.88%). Fulfillment, returns and marketing consume most of the gross margin.

## 2. How customer acquisition costs changed, how that drove the DTC shakeout, and which models survived

### Takeaway
Apple's App Tracking Transparency (iOS 14.5, April 2021) weakened Meta's targeting and measurement. Meta itself estimated a roughly $10B revenue hit for 2022. Combined with years of rising ad prices (Meta's average price per ad was still rising 9–10% a year in 2025), this broke the 2015–2020 DTC model of venture-funded, paid-social-led growth. Most of the high-profile DTC IPOs and SPACs then collapsed or sold below their IPO price: Casper went private at $6.90 against a $12 IPO price, Blue Apron sold for about $103M, and Allbirds sold its assets for about $39M after a $7.1B pre-IPO valuation. The survivors are profitable omnichannel operators (Warby Parker), community-led brands investing in stores (Gymshark), and capital-efficient brands with a disciplined paid-social engine plus wholesale (True Classic).

### Cited Findings

CFO Dave Wehner called this "an estimate," saying the changes made ad targeting and outcome measurement harder and raised the cost of driving outcomes. — [CNBC, Feb 2, 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/02/02/facebook-says-apple-ios-privacy-change-will-cost-10-billion-this-year.html) **[Trade press reporting company statement]**

- Adore Me reported that its iOS CPM rose 19.5% after the ATT update, versus 17.4% on Android. — [Marketing Dive, 2021](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/were-going-back-to-the-drawing-board-dtc-brands-brace-for-more-anti-tra/606607) **[Trade press, company anecdote]** (search summary)
- The founder of Disco, a skincare DTC brand, reported that CAC rose about 300% in April 2021 after iOS 14.5. — reported in trade press, surfaced alongside [Marketing Brew, Oct 2022](https://www.marketingbrew.com/stories/2022/10/06/dtc-advertisers-once-reliant-on-facebook-and-instagram-are-looking-at-different-and-less-expensive-marketing-platforms) **[Anecdote; I did not confirm which article carries it]**

### Inferences
- The shakeout hit hardest where three things combined: low repeat frequency (mattresses, sneakers bought every year or two), high fixed CAC dependence, and venture-scale growth targets. Meal kits failed for a different reason. They had high frequency but high early churn and thin food margins; Recharge's food and beverage first-renewal churn of 28.2% is consistent with this.
- The survivors share four traits: profitability or a clear path to it, an omnichannel footprint where stores act as acquisition and fit-trial channels (Warby Parker, Gymshark, True Classic), community or creator-led demand that lowers blended CAC (Gymshark), and a disciplined testing machine for paid social rather than brute-force spend (True Classic).
- Allbirds shows that stores are not a cure on their own. Retail expansion without product-led repeat demand added fixed cost and sped up the decline.
- Rising Meta ad prices (+9–10% a year in 2025) mean a brand's contribution margin per first order keeps falling unless AOV, conversion or repeat rate rise to offset it.

The L.E.K. report on rising DTC CAC was surfaced but not read: [L.E.K.](https://www.lek.com/insights/direct-selling/fighting-rising-direct-consumer-customer-acquisition-costs)

- The McKinsey and BCG DTC reports suggested in the brief were not retrieved.

## 3. The role of social commerce and creators (TikTok Shop, Instagram) and which categories sell best on TikTok Shop

### Takeaway
TikTok Shop became a top-tier US ecommerce channel within about two years of its September 2023 launch: an estimated $14–16B in US GMV in 2025, larger than Wayfair, Etsy or eBay by third-party estimates, and about $23.4B forecast for 2026. Beauty and personal care leads (about 19–22.5% of GMV), with womenswear second (about 12.5%) and menswear third (about 8%). Fashion overall (women's, men's and shoes) is about 24%. Discovery is creator-driven, and sales skew toward low-price, impulse items (apparel sweet spot $5–20).

### Cited Findings
- TikTok Shop had more than $14B in US sales in 2025, making it bigger than Wayfair, Etsy and eBay. It had about $6.75B in US sales from January to April 2026, nearly double the year-earlier period. TikTok does not disclose sales. — [Modern Retail, May 19, 2026 (citing Charm.io)](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/tiktok-shop-says-sales-from-u-s-small-businesses-climbed-66-in-2025/) **[Third-party estimate]**
- 2025 US category shares: beauty and personal care about 19% (about $2.7B); womenswear about 12.5% (about $1.8B). — [Modern Retail / Charm.io](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/tiktok-shop-says-sales-from-u-s-small-businesses-climbed-66-in-2025/) **[Third-party estimate]**
- 2025 US GMV was $15.82B (eMarketer), with $23.4B forecast for 2026 (+48%). Within $11.06B of category-tracked GMV (Charm.io via AfterShip), shares were:
  - beauty and personal care: 22.5% ($2.49B)
  - womenswear and underwear: 12.6% ($1.39B)
  - menswear and underwear: 8.1% ($890M)
  - food and beverages: 6.9% ($759.8M)
  - home supplies: 4.1% ($457.5M)
  - shoes: 3.6% ($400M)
  - Fashion combined is about 24%.
  - Source: [Eightx, May 30, 2026](https://eightx.co/blog/average-ecommerce-tiktok-shop-revenue-share-by-vertical-2026) **[Vendor compilation of third-party estimates]**
- In Eightx's client observations, TikTok Shop typically reaches 5–15% of revenue for mid-market DTC brands once the program is built, and 20% or more for beauty and impulse-supplement brands. — [Eightx](https://eightx.co/blog/average-ecommerce-tiktok-shop-revenue-share-by-vertical-2026) **[Vendor observation, not a dataset]**

## 4. What specifically makes apparel winners win (core basics and repeat purchase vs drops and scarcity)

### Takeaway
The apparel winners cluster into a few repeatable models:
- **Core basics bought on repeat:** True Classic (men's tees, then retail), and Skims (shapewear and basics, $5B valuation).
- **Community-led activewear:** Gymshark.
- **Factory-direct "quality basics at low prices":** Quince ($10.1B valuation).
- **Test-and-reorder at extreme speed:** Shein (100–200 unit test batches).

What they share is fit consistency on a core product, data-driven inventory (test small, reorder winners), and a demand engine beyond paid ads (creators or celebrity, community, wholesale and stores). I found little sourced evidence that scarcity drops alone build durable apparel businesses. The winners use newness on top of an always-available core.

### Inferences
- **The core basic is the retention engine.** A customer who has found their size in a fit-consistent tee can reorder without fit risk. This tackles apparel's two weak points: returns of about 23–25%, and repeat purchase of about 24–26%. True Classic's wholesale is built on the core tee rather than newness, which suggests the core is what earns repeat and retail sell-through.
- **Drops work best as an overlay.** Shein and Quince show that the scalable version of "drops" is high-frequency small-batch testing with fast reorders of winners, not artificial scarcity. Their newness is data collection. For a small brand, limited drops can serve as low-risk demand tests and as reasons for existing customers to come back.
- **A non-paid demand engine is common to all winners:** celebrity or founder (Skims), community and athletes (Gymshark), price-value word of mouth (Quince), creators and short video (TikTok Shop sellers). True Classic is the paid-social exception, and it relied on unusual testing discipline and is now adding brand marketing and wholesale.

## 5. Implications for a small DTC apparel brand in 2026 (channel mix, retention, margin, returns)

The brand in question is Shelby: an always-in-stock core tee plus small product drops, with traffic mainly from short-form social video, built on Medusa + Next.js.

### Takeaway
The evidence favors a core-basic retention model over a hype-drop model. Shelby should:
- Protect a high gross margin (apparel benchmark about 57%), because about 19–25% of online apparel revenue is at risk of returns and Meta ad prices are still rising about 10% a year.
- Make the core tee's fit consistent and reorderable, so the second order carries no fit risk.
- Use small drops as data-driven demand tests and reasons to return, in the style of Shein and Quince.
- Treat short-form creators and TikTok Shop (5–15% of revenue for mid-market DTC) as the main non-CPM acquisition channel.
- Defer wholesale and stores until the brand has scale.

### Cited Findings
These are the supporting data points from the sections above:
- Apparel gross margin is about 56.88%, but net margin is only 3.85% at public apparel companies. — [Damodaran Jan 2026](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/margin.html) **[Independent]**
- The online return rate is about 19.3% (2025 estimate). Apparel-specific figures are 23.2% (Loop, 2024) and 24.4% (Coresight, 2023). — [NRF 2025](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-expected-to-return-nearly-850-billion-in-merchandise-in-2025) **[Commissioned]**; [Loop](https://info.loopreturns.com/hubfs/2024%20Winter%20Benchmark%20Report.pdf) **[Vendor]**

- Loss per new customer rose from $9 (2013) to $29 (2022), while profit per repeat sale rose from $28 to $39. — [SimplicityDX/Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220719005425/en/Brands-Losing-a-Record-29-for-Each-New-Customer-Acquired) **[Vendor]**

### Inferences
These are recommendations derived from the evidence. They are not sourced facts.
1. **Margin:** target landed-cost gross margin at or above the apparel benchmark (about 55–60%) on the core tee. Model contribution margin after returns of about 20–25%, shipping and payment fees. Price promotions erode the margin buffer that funds CAC. Multipacks and bundles raise AOV without cutting unit price, and they suit the TikTok Shop price sweet spot if the per-unit price falls toward about $20.
2. **Returns:** fit is the main return driver in apparel. Invest early in a precise size chart, fit notes, try-on video (which doubles as short-form content), and "exchange first" return flows. On Medusa this means exchanges or store credit rather than refunds, and capturing the customer's size for one-click reorders. Bracketing is common (more than half of consumers by vendor estimates), so a free-exchange, paid-refund policy is a lever to test.
3. **Retention:** the core tee should be the replenishment product. Reorder prompts, "your size" saved in the account, and an optional tee-refill subscription or club are options; Recharge's thin-base apparel churn hints this can work. Use drops to give existing customers reasons to come back through email/SMS and early access. Because subscription churn peaks at renewals 2–3, the first reorder experience matters most.
4. **Channel mix:** short-form organic video plus creator and affiliate seeding is the lowest-CAC channel for a small brand in 2026. Add TikTok Shop for discovery and conversion (benchmark: 5–15% of revenue). Use Meta paid only with a disciplined creative-testing system (the True Classic lesson), and retarget with first-party data from the Medusa store, since ATT reduced third-party signal. Defer wholesale and owned stores until scale is proven (the "nine figures" bar; the Allbirds caution).
5. **Drops vs core:** keep the core tee always in stock, because stockouts break the reorder habit and waste paid traffic. Run drops as small batches (the Shein/Quince test-and-reorder logic at small-brand scale) to limit inventory risk, and promote drop winners into the core range.
6. **Measurement:** track new-customer CAC, first-order contribution margin, 90/180-day repeat rate, return and exchange rate by SKU and size, and creator-attributed revenue. Category benchmarks are vendor-sourced and noisy, so the brand's own cohorts matter more than published averages.

- No sourced evidence was found on the economics of multipacks or tee subscription programs.
